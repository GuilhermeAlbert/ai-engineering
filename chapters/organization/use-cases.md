# Module 4: AI Across the Organization


This module maps generative AI use cases function by function, then teaches a repeatable way to judge whether a use case is actually worth pursuing.

## Marketing and CX: personalization, content generation, service automation

GenAI lets marketing teams produce content variants at a scale manual work can't match (product descriptions, ad copy, email variants) and lets customer-experience (CX) teams deploy chatbots that handle a first tier of support conversationally.

**Practical example:** An e-commerce brand generates 500 localized product-description variants (one per SKU per market) from a single structured product-data feed, instead of briefing a copywriter for each one: while a human still reviews a sample for brand-voice compliance.

## Operations: workflow automation, documentation, procurement, supply chain

Operations teams use GenAI to draft standard operating procedures, summarize supplier contracts, generate RFP language, and flag anomalies in supply-chain reporting narratives.

**Practical example:** A procurement team feeds a supplier's proposed contract into an AI tool and asks it to flag clauses that deviate from the company's standard terms: cutting first-pass legal review time, though a lawyer still makes the final call.

## HR and Talent: recruiting, onboarding, learning and development, performance

HR applications include drafting job descriptions, generating interview questions from a role's competency framework, building onboarding-plan drafts, and summarizing 360-degree feedback into a first-draft performance narrative.

**Practical example:** A hiring manager asks an AI tool to generate five behavioral interview questions targeting "conflict resolution" for a mid-level engineering role: then edits and selects the two best, rather than starting from a blank page.

## Finance: reporting, forecasting, compliance summaries

Finance teams use GenAI to draft narrative sections of financial reports (turning numbers into readable commentary), summarize regulatory-filing changes, and produce first-draft variance explanations.

**Practical example:** A financial analyst pastes a spreadsheet of budget-vs-actual figures and asks the model to draft the "explanation of variances" paragraph for the monthly report, then verifies every number against source data before it goes out, since the model can misstate figures (see [How Generative AI Works](../models/how-generative-ai-works.md)).

## Product and Innovation: ideation, prototyping, market research

Product teams use GenAI to rapidly generate and filter feature-idea lists, draft user-story text, mock up UI copy, and synthesize competitor/market-research documents into digestible briefs.

**Practical example:** A product manager asks an AI tool to generate 30 feature-name and tagline variants for a new app feature, then picks 3 to test with users: compressing a brainstorm that used to take a full workshop into 20 minutes of drafting plus human curation.

## Leadership and Strategy: competitive intelligence, board communication, scenario planning

At the leadership layer, GenAI helps synthesize large volumes of competitor news and analyst reports into digestible intelligence briefs, draft board-ready communication, and rapidly sketch out multiple "what if" scenarios for planning discussions.

**Practical example:** Ahead of a strategy offsite, a CEO's chief of staff uses an AI tool to summarize the last quarter's competitor announcements across five companies into a single one-page brief, freeing the leadership team's time for discussion instead of research.

## Case studies: Klarna, Coca-Cola, JPMorgan, and others

The course uses named, real-world organizational examples (Klarna, Coca-Cola, JPMorgan, and others) to illustrate value creation, ethical trade-offs, governance requirements, and deployment realities in practice: not as an endorsement, but as case material.

**Illustrative pattern (typical of this kind of case study):** A financial-services firm deploys an internal GenAI assistant for relationship managers to draft client-meeting prep notes, reporting significant time savings: while also having to build a review layer to ensure no client-specific figures are hallucinated before the notes are used in a live meeting. This pairing of "value created" with "governance built to control the new risk" is the recurring pattern the case studies are meant to teach.

## Use-case evaluation: assessing value, risk, and organizational readiness

Not every plausible AI idea is worth building. The course teaches evaluating each candidate use case along three axes: **value** (time saved, revenue enabled, quality improved), **risk** (what happens if the output is wrong, and how exposed/regulated is the domain), and **organizational readiness** (do we have the data, the process ownership, and the change-management capacity to actually deploy this).

**Practical example:** "Auto-drafting internal meeting notes" scores high value / low risk / high readiness: a good first pilot. "Fully automated loan-approval decisions" might score high value but high risk and low readiness (regulatory exposure, explainability requirements): a poor first pilot, better suited for a much later, more governed phase.
