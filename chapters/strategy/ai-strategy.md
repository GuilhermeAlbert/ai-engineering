# Module 8: Creating an AI Strategy


This module synthesizes the previous seven sections into a concrete organizational AI strategy.

## Review of the AI strategy framework and integration of all concepts

By this point, a participant should be able to connect: capability literacy (Module 2) → prompting skill (Module 3) → cross-functional use cases (Module 4) → risk/governance guardrails (Module 5) → organizational readiness (Module 6) → economic context (Module 7) into a single coherent plan, rather than treating each module as an isolated topic.

**Practical example:** A strategy that proposes an AI-powered contract-review tool (a Module 4 use case) is incomplete unless it also specifies which governance checklist applies (Module 5), whether legal has the readiness to adopt it (Module 6), and what capability tier (LLM alone, or LLM+RAG against the contract repository: Module 2) the use case actually requires.

## Using the AI opportunity canvas to map use cases to business value

An **AI opportunity canvas** is a one-page structured template: typically capturing: the use case, the business value it targets, the data/capability it requires, the risk tier, and the owner: used to compare many candidate AI initiatives on equal footing rather than pursuing whichever one a senior stakeholder happens to be excited about this month.

**Practical example canvas entry:**

| Use case | Business value | Capability needed | Risk tier | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI-drafted first-pass customer support replies | Reduce average handle time | LLM + RAG (knowledge base) | Medium (customer-facing) | Head of CX |

Filling this out for 10–15 candidate ideas turns "what should we do with AI" from a debate into a comparable dataset.

## Applying an effort-impact-risk matrix to prioritize AI initiatives

An **effort-impact-risk matrix** plots each candidate use case (often from the opportunity canvas above) along three axes to identify: **quick wins** (high impact, low effort, low risk: do these first), **strategic bets** (high impact, high effort: plan and resource carefully), and **avoid/defer** (low impact and/or high risk relative to effort).

**Practical example:** "AI-assisted meeting-note summarization" plots as high impact / low effort / low risk → quick win, launch this quarter. "Fully automated credit-decisioning" plots as high impact / high effort / high risk → a multi-year strategic bet requiring the governance maturity from Module 5, not a first pilot.

## Building an AI roadmap integrating quick wins, mid-term initiatives, and long-term transformation

A roadmap sequences initiatives across a timeline (e.g., 0–3 months, 3–12 months, 1–3 years), explicitly using quick wins to build organizational credibility and momentum that fund and de-risk the larger, longer-term initiatives.

**Practical example:** Quarter 1: launch AI-assisted meeting notes and prompt-library training (quick wins, cheap, visible). Year 1: roll out AI-assisted contract review with a defined governance checklist (mid-term). Year 2–3: evaluate a fully AI-assisted underwriting pipeline once governance, data infrastructure, and organizational trust have matured (long-term transformation).

## Crafting the business case for AI investment and how to pitch it effectively

A credible AI business case quantifies expected value (time saved × hourly cost, or revenue enabled), states the investment required (tooling, training, change management: not just software licensing), and is explicit about the risk being taken on and how it's mitigated: rather than leading with "AI is strategically important," which funds nothing on its own.

**Practical example:** Instead of "we need an AI strategy to stay competitive," a strong business case states: "This pilot costs $80K over 6 months, is projected to save 3,000 support-agent hours/year (~$150K), and its main risk: incorrect responses to customers: is mitigated by a RAG-grounded knowledge base and mandatory review of any escalation before sending."

## Presenting your AI strategy to boards, investors, and skeptical stakeholders

Board and investor audiences want risk-adjusted, business-language framing, not technical detail: what value, by when, at what cost, with what safeguards, benchmarked against what competitors are doing. Skeptical stakeholders specifically need their likely objections (job impact, reliability, cost) addressed proactively rather than left for Q&A.

**Practical example:** A CIO presenting to a skeptical board opens not with model architecture, but with: "Our top competitor already automated 40% of first-line support drafting; here's our plan to match that within two quarters, here's the guardrail that prevents customer-facing errors, and here's what happens to the support team's role: augmented, not eliminated."

## Capstone project workshop: developing and peer-reviewing your organizational AI strategy

The capstone deliverable combines the opportunity map, roadmap, owners, risks, dependencies, and metrics developed throughout this chapter. Structured peer review pressure-tests the plan against blind spots a single author would not catch alone.

## Final reflection: the kind of AI leader you want to become

The course closes on a deliberately non-technical note: given everything covered: capability, risk, economics, organizational change: what kind of leader do you intend to be in this transition? Cautious skeptic, aggressive early adopter, careful builder of trust and guardrails? There's no single correct answer, but the course's premise is that this choice, made deliberately, shapes outcomes more than any single tool choice does.
